# Narrative and visualisation rationale

## Central question

**Where is Australian wildlife protected, what do the available indicators say about biodiversity, and where is the evidence incomplete or uneven?**

The project avoids treating protection, ecosystem condition, species trends and monitoring activity as interchangeable measures. It moves from the geography of protected areas to land cover, wildlife indicators and finally the evidence base used to describe biodiversity.

## Story sequence

### 1\. Where is protection?

**M1 — Where protection sits: records versus land area**

The opening map introduces the protected-area system through CAPAD 2024 records grouped by state or territory, with optional point markers. It sets up a key distinction: a count of database records is not the same as unique protected places, and neither record counts nor summed area alone measures how effectively biodiversity is protected.

### 2\. What land is being protected?

**C4 — Grassland and herbaceous cropland over time**

This chart compares reported land-cover shares from 1988 to 2024. It is intended to show how the composition of land cover has shifted over time. Because the values are proportions, an increase in one category affects the shares of other categories. Unusual year-to-year changes should be checked against the original data and methodology rather than attributed to a cause without evidence.

**C5 — Natural woody vegetation by state and territory**

This comparison highlights the distribution of natural woody vegetation area. It adds a land-cover perspective to M1, but area should not be read as a direct measure of ecological condition, intactness or legal protection.

### 3\. Is wildlife doing well?

**C6 — Threatened Species Index trend**

The index provides a relative trend for the monitored group against a baseline. It helps communicate change over time, but it is not a census of every threatened species or individual animal.

**C7 — Waterbird survey counts over time**

The chart presents reported waterbird counts by survey year. Survey coverage and methods can differ, and a large count in a particular year may reflect survey scope as well as ecological conditions. The series should not automatically be interpreted as a continuous population estimate.

**M3 — Threatened species associated with protected-area locations**

The proportional-symbol map links CAPAD locations to the threatened-species count of the containing IBRA region. This provides a spatial view of regional context, not park-level species observations. Multiple park markers in the same region can therefore share the same regional count.

### 4\. Can we trust what we see?

**M2 — Wildlife records by natural region**

The interactive IBRA map lets readers explore available occurrence summaries by dataset, measure, status and period. It foregrounds the geography of recorded evidence. A percentage of occurrence records inside protected areas is not the percentage of land protected, and aggregated records are not equivalent to animal abundance.

**C8 — Major biodiversity monitoring data sources**

This chart compares the volume of records associated with monitoring resources. It describes the distribution of records across sources, not the distribution or abundance of wildlife.

**C9 — Monitoring effort by IBRA region**

This chart shows how the supplied regional monitoring records are distributed geographically. A strong concentration in one region can reflect uneven monitoring and reporting coverage; it should not be interpreted as a direct ranking of biodiversity.

**C10 — Exotic environmental pest risk profiles**

The searchable pest explorer presents assessment fields for listed exotic environmental pests. The assessment dimensions should remain distinct: a risk category or environmental-impact rating is not proof that a pest is currently present or causing an outbreak.

## Closing message

Where land is protected, where species are changing, and where observations are collected are related but different questions. Read together, the maps and charts can show patterns and gaps in the available evidence, but their conclusions depend on the scope, definitions and coverage of the underlying datasets.
