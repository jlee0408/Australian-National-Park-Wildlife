Protected, But Not Everywhere

What Australian data actually tells us about where wildlife is protected, how it is changing, and where the evidence is thin.

How to read this page

Koalas, quolls and kookaburras all depend on protected land. This page asks one question: is Australia’s wildlife actually safe inside its protected areas? We look at where protected areas are, what land they hold, how threatened species are doing, and how far we can trust the data.

A “record” means one entry in a database, such as a park listing or a bird sighting. It is not a count of animals.

M1. Where protection sits: records versus land area

Tasmania has the most mapped records, while Western Australia accounts for the largest summed area.

Map extent: Australia · mainland and Tasmania

State shading counts database entries assigned to each state or territory; point markers show CAPAD coordinates. The map displays 14,471 mapped records. The source dataset contains 14,492 records overall, including external-territory records not shown on this state map.

What this means: Tasmania has 4,236 of the 14,471 mapped records (29.2%), despite being one of Australia’s smallest states. Western Australia accounts for about 79.5 million hectares (45.2%) of the summed area. This contrast is consistent with many smaller reserves in Tasmania and fewer, much larger protected areas in Western Australia. Markers cluster along the east coast, in Victoria, Tasmania and the south-west, where population and reporting are also concentrated. Caution: overlapping boundaries can inflate summed area, and record counts are not a unique-park count or a precise measure of the land that is effectively protected.

What land is being protected?

Area and land-cover composition describe the physical landscape, but neither one tells us whether ecosystems are healthy or adequately protected.

What this means: Queensland holds about 38.4 million hectares, or 33.5% of the reported national total of 114.5 million hectares. New South Wales and Western Australia follow; together, the three states account for roughly three-quarters of the total. The Australian Capital Territory contributes very little by comparison. Land-cover composition from 1988 to 2024, shown as proportions rather than raw area.

Loading C4…

What this means: grassland rose from 36.9% in 1988 to 44.2% in 2024, while herbaceous crops fell from 17.8% to 8.8%. Because the classes represent shares of total land cover, an increase in one class changes the share available to others. Grassland’s jump to about 64% in 2011 was largely reversed in 2012. Caution: focus on the long-term pattern rather than a single-year spike; verify the source’s explanation before attributing the 2011–2012 change to weather.

CHAPTER 3

Is wildlife doing well?

A species index and survey counts can reveal important signals, but their coverage and survey methods matter.

06

C6. Threatened Species Index trend

Index trend for threatened terrestrial species, with the supplied lower and upper bounds.

Loading C6…

What this means: the Threatened Species Index starts at 1.0 in 1985, peaks at about 1.3 in 1987, and falls to 0.33 by 2021—a decline of about 67% from baseline. The decline is steepest up to the mid-2000s and flatter after around 2008, without a return to baseline. The uncertainty band shows that the exact level is less certain than the overall downward direction. Caution: this is a relative index for monitored species, not a count of every threatened species or every individual animal.

Data limitation: D1 provides park locations, D2 provides species records by IBRA region, and D3 provides threatened classifications. Since D2 does not identify species observations within individual parks, each park marker inherits the count for its containing IBRA region. This is a regional proxy, not a park-specific survey.

What this means: for 2016–2020, Sydney Basin has 179 threatened species and South Eastern Queensland has 168, followed by NSW North Coast (109) and South Eastern Highlands (106). Of the 14,471 mapped locations, 13,248 (91.5%) fall inside a matched region; 118 species names are classified as threatened in the supplied summary. Caution: the regional count is repeated at every park location in that region. It shows regional concentration, not a survey of species living inside each individual park.

Can we trust what we see?

The remaining maps and charts show the evidence base itself: where observations come from, how monitoring is distributed, and what pest assessments mean.

Explore occurrence summaries across Australia’s 89 IBRA natural regions using the dataset, measure, status and period controls.

Loading IBRA7_regions.geojson and D2 summary CSV files…

Protection status metrics are sums across the three protection-status CSV parts. Threatened/introduced records can be filtered by status and source year period. If “All periods” is selected, record counts are summed across periods; species counts across periods are not unique species totals.

What this means: the supplied summary reports 26.7% of occurrence records inside protected areas overall. Darker regions appear along the east coast and south-east, while the arid interior is lighter. This likely reflects where people live, visit and record wildlife as well as ecological differences. Caution: 26.7% is a share of records, not the share of land protected. With “All periods” selected, counts are summed across periods and are not unique-species totals. Confirm the selected measure before interpreting regional comparisons.

THE BIG PICTURE

Where data is collected, where land is protected, and where species are at risk are three different maps

Protected-area records are concentrated differently from protected land area. Threatened species cluster in particular natural regions, while the Threatened Species Index has fallen by about 67% since 1985. At the same time, monitoring evidence leans heavily on a few data sources and geographic areas.

The evidence suggests that protection on a map is not enough on its own. We also need to know whether habitats remain in good condition, whether wildlife indicators improve, and whether monitoring is broad enough to show what is happening across Australia. Better and more balanced monitoring would make it easier to judge whether protection is working.
